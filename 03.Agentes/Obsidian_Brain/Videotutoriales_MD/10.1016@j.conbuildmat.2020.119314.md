##### 1. Introduction 

Soil based materials (SBMs) have been widely used for building constructions around the glove. However, the development of more economical construction materials with satisfactory performance in terms of mechanical response, durability and maintenance, among others, led to replace SBMs. Nevertheless, developed countries such as Germany, France or UK certainly show an important number of earth-based buildings (e.g. the UK has 500,000 earth-based dwellings) [1,2]. Besides, low- and middleincome economies show historical and even residential buildings

made by using SBMs (e.g. Peru, Chile, and Colombia, among others) [3,4]. Currently, it is estimated that 30% of the world’s population lives in buildings made of adobes [5]. In spite of there are several SBM techniques (e.g. rammed earth, cob or clom, wattle and daub, wytchert, etc.), adobe is the most preferred due to the large-scale production availability which significantly reduces costs and keeps consistent quality. Adobe is also named as mud brick, soil block or compressed earth block, among others. All these terms describe masonry units made by pressing a soil mix blend (i.e. clay, silt, sand, coarse aggregates and water). These so-formed blocks are only dried, rather than fired, and they are laid up into the wall by using earth mortar, instead of cement mortar [6]. As a result, modern architecture is paying more and more attention to these environmentally friendly construction materials [7] to address

growing concerns regarding the carbon and environmental footprint of fired bricks and cement based products. At this point, it appears that the use of adobes might be a contribution toward sustainable construction although some drawbacks must be certainly highlighted. 

Adobe shows low compressive strength (i.e. from 0.6 to 7 MPa), low tensile strength (i.e. approx. 10% of compressive strength) [8] and water resistance which leads also to highly reducing strength when adobe is saturated with water, even up to the complete decohesion [9]. These issues have been faced by mechanical compaction (i.e. higher pressure conformation), chemical stabilization (e.g. cement, lime, bitumen etc) and fibers inclusions such as hemp straw [10] and fonio (Digitaria exilis) straw [11], among others [12]. In spite of chemical stabilization has been widely applied, the use of cement has been discouraged lately due to its environmental impact. Conversely, other more environmentally friendly stabilizers are being investigated (e.g. fly ash, ground granulated blast furnace slag, silica fume, etc.) [2,13]. Alternatively, fiber reinforcement may achieve similar stabilization by creating an internal net that reinforces adobes and keeps particles stuck [14,15]. Thus, some authors have reported the enhancement of flexural strength (i.e. up to 80%) [10], compressive strength (i.e. up to 95%) [16], water resistance [11,17] and even thermal conductivity which might be reduced up to 60% [10,11]. 

Most of reported studies have been developed by using natural fibers from agro-industrial wastes (e.g. coconut husk, sugarcane residue, oil palm fruit residue) [18–21] which are economically and widely produced. Conversely, these residues are quite difficult to stock due to seasonality and they are already traded in other markets (e.g. animal feed, household fuels, etc. . .) making the business model difficult for brick producers. At this point, the paper and pulp industry has been identified as a potential source of fiber 

that may contribute to further reducing the environmental footprint since industrial waste generally has a larger footprint than agro-waste, from a life cycle assessment perspective [22]. 

In addition, PPR’s waste management legislation has been extensively revised, forcing producers to seek novel strategies. Through legislation, the landfill option is firmly restricted, and on-site landfills owned by paper-producing companies are being rapidly phased out. However, due to the large amount of waste, the recovery methods are simply too expensive and their environmental impact is uncertain [23]. 

Thus, this research aims to assess the technological feasibility of using PPR for adobe reinforcement by considering mandatory standards and industrial procedures to allow direct and effective scaling. 

Actually, PPR has been used to replace clay in order to produce lightweight fired clay bricks [24–26]. PPR burns during the firing process and the clay matrix increases its porosity, leading to reductions in compressive strength and thermal conductivity. However, as stated above, the higher energy intensity of fired clay brick manufacturing leads us to assess the feasibility of using unfired clay bricks (i.e. the adobe). 

Therefore, this paper firstly shows the materials used and their properties, in terms of mineral, chemical, and physical composition. Then, methods of making samples are described and mechanical, physical, and thermal tests discussed.

##### 3. Test setup 

Raw materials were characterized to determine their mineralogical composition by means of X-ray diffraction (XRD) and their chemical composition by X-ray fluorescence (XRF). The raw clay particle size distribution was determined by means of the laser diffraction technique, which is able to measure particles from 10 nm up to 3.5 mm. In addition, the maximal particle size of PPR was limited to 1.5 mm by sieving the raw PPR. 

Each specimen was measured and weighed at every stage (i.e. from green to dried samples) in accordance with UNE-EN 77216:2011 [30] and UNE-EN 772-13:2000 [31]. From these measurements, the linear shrinkage (lS) from green to dry samples and the bulk density were determined. 

The test for determining the water behaviour, the so-called Swinburne accelerated erosion test (SAET), was carried out in accordance with UNE 41410:2008 [32]. This test was performed as follows: specimens were tilted by 27� relative to the vertical plane and water drops from a 5 mm diameter hole placed 1 m above the middle point of the sample’s exposed surface. After 10 min, the hole produced was measured using a 3 mm diameter bar. When the depth is greater than 10 mm, the adobe was classified as non-valid for severe weathering. 

Thermal diffusivity and thermal conductivity were determined based on the transient plane source (TPS) method in accordance with ISO 22007-2 [33]. The power heat source was set between 80 and 150 W and the time between 20 and 80 s so that the thermal wave did not reach the outer sample boundaries during the 

transient recording and also limited the characteristic time values [34]. It must be mentioned that regardless of material heterogeneity, when thermal parameters are determined by measurements rather than calculations, differences that may lead to inaccurate comparisons are shown. Based on the deep review carried out by previous authors [31], thermal conductivity of adobe is usually measured by hot wire apparatus or a guarded hot plate. The first uses a transient method but the hot wire must be totally embedded in the sample so that no edge effect can affect the measurement. In addition, the heat flow meter or guarded hot plate equipment works under steady-state conditions, but the contact between the hot and cold plates must be perfectly adjusted to the open pore surface material (i.e. commonly by using some thin films) since the hypothesis of perfect thermal contact must be checked. In case of an irregular contact, the air trapped between surfaces incorporates convective phases which lead to a large decrease in the measured thermal conductivity. The modulus of rupture and compressive strength were determined by means of a universal machine. This equipment incorporates a 15 kN capacity load cell while the loading rate settings are controlled by software. The compressive strength was determined in both halves of each prismatic specimen, after breaking them to determine the modulus of rupture. As mentioned above [35], there is a lack of comprehensive standards, related to earth construction, particularly to adobe construction. Thus, in the absence of specific UNE standards for adobes, UNE 41410:2008 [32] was followed for compressive test. On the other hand, the determination of modulus of rupture was based on UNE 67042:1988 [36]. 

Furthermore, the surface area, pore volume, and diameter were measured by means of nitrogen adsorption/desorption cycles at 573 K and the microstructure of the fired samples was assessed by using scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipment incorporating an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS) device working at 20 kV and approx. 20 Pa. 

As stated in the Introduction, PPR may be considered a hazardous waste with a significant environmental impact. Therefore, the leaching behaviour of samples must be addressed with the aim of assessing its potential impact on the environment. Thus duplicate batch leaching tests were carried out in accordance with US EPA (1992) [37] and the corresponding concentrations in the filtrate were analysed by means of inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) equipment.

Furthermore, the fibers also play a major role regarding the mechanical response when PPR is added. Therefore, despite the fact that the control samples clearly show a brittle behavior (Fig. 8a), the addition of PPR fibers increases the capacity of the masonry to support additional load after the first crack and the ultimate strain leading to a more ductile behavior before collapse (Fig. 8b) [46]. Hence, the stress-strain curves may be considered similar to those shown by quasi-brittle materials [12] which has led several authors to successfully assess pseudo-ductile model by means of a bilinear idealization of the experimental stress– strain curves [47] or even a third order polynomial formulation [48]. Such simplifications should be considered to be based on several assumptions which are beyond the scope (e.g. strain ductility is just a fictitious parameter which is used to define an upper bound to inelastic strains for quasi brittle materials, ultimate strength cannot exceed the actual compressive strength of bricks, etc.). 

The compressive strength is greatly increased (i.e. from 3.4 to 10.7 MPa) when the amount of PPR is increased. It is suggested that the increase of the compressive strength is related to the homogeneous microstructure because of the presence of the PPR fibers net, which reduces the porosity of adobe, as stated by previous authors [20]. These authors demonstrated that the smaller the fiber size, the better the mechanical response of adobes, it was explained through the degradation of the soil matrix due to the bulky fibers of larger sizes. In addition, this explanation is corroborated by considering that the smaller fibers mix more easily with the soil, as might be observed by SEM. Hence, in this case it is suggested that the combination of clay and fibers forms a paste surrounding the silt grains, which contributes to a reduction in the compressive effect by dissipating the energy in plastic deformation [49]. This claim is also in accordance with the higher obtained shrinkage, since samples also undergo stress during the drying process. The deviation from the results increases with the addition of PPR, confirming that the inherent inhomogeneity and natural randomness of earthen materials are greatly affected by such addition. Although the samples’ manufacturing process was well defined and controlled for every series, the increase of PPR leads to modification of the extrusion pressure, and the distribution of fibers is more heterogeneous, leading to a decrease in the accuracy of the values obtained. 

In addition, it must be noted that the results were obtained from dried samples (i.e. after drying in an oven at 100 �C). Only a partial increase of moisture can be considered during the short time between getting the samples out of the oven and placing them in the universal machine. The dried clay tends to recover the so-called equilibrium moisture, which is defined as the condition in which equal amounts of absorbed and desorbed water molecules are exchanged by the material (i.e. between 0.5 and 7%) [31]. 

Previous authors [51] have studied the effect of moisture in adobes in terms of mechanical response. In accordance with such findings, the interstitial water content at meso-scale leads to the exertion of a normal force on the blend layers when these are approximated or separated (i.e. the so-called Stefan adhesion prin- 

ciple). Therefore it is suggested that the effect of such low moisture content in the results obtained from mechanical assays may be considered negligible. As aforementioned, the addition of PPR leads to a more plastic response which can be observed by considering the increase of the modulus of rupture. Although the increase in ductile behaviour may be considered as an enhancement from the point of view of mechanical response, it is necessary to pay attention to the compatibility of connections between structural elements in order to assure that the deformations are compatible. 

In addition to the required adobe mechanical response for masonries at building scale, thermal conductivity is a key factor regarding the building energy performance. According to Zhang et al. (2016) [52], there are fewer researches on the thermal performance of adobe buildings in comparison with other building materials, and it was pointed out that this issue reduces the promotion of adobe. 

The main advantage of using adobe, in terms of building thermal performance, is its specific heat capacity, which leads to moderate temperature swings in buildings. Specific heat capacity characterizes the ability of a material to store thermal energy. Hence, heat is absorbed by the adobe material during the day due to the solar exposure, while during the night the adobe releases the absorbed heat. In this way, the interior zones are kept cooler during the day and warmer during the night. The specific heat capacity usually ranges between 1.4 [53] and 0.9 MJ m<sup>�3</sup> K<sup>�1</sup> [54] for a dB of approximately 1800 kg m<sup>�3</sup> . As long as the amount of PPR is increased, the specific heat capacity decreases, which means that lower energy is required to change the adobe temperature and therefore the better thermal comfort assumed for adobe masonries is reduced (Fig. 9). 

Conversely, thermal conductivity is commonly higher than that of fired clay bricks or even concrete blocks [55] but the addition of PPR leads to a reduction in its value. ##### 4.3. Water erosion and toxicity 

In spite of the enhanced properties in terms of mechanical and thermal behaviour, the addition of KP does not avoid problems related to water. Clay layers absorb water quickly and separate from each other, leading to an expansion of the adobe brick and washing out large amounts of materials. At this point, it must be considered that PPR fibers help to reduce this last effect but certainly do not provide an effective solution. Thus, the use of adobe for masonries building must take into account additional measures to waterproof the adobe masonry. The series showed an acceptable response to SAET test up to 12.5% of addition. The observed average depth of the erosion, for specimens that meet the requirements (i.e. lower than 10 mm), was 6.5, 9.5, 7.5, 8.5 and 10 mm, for samples with 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10 and 12.5% PPR. Control samples showed an average depth of 4.5 cm which is even lower than the average values obtained by previous authors [56] for stabilized adobes. Thus, the measured depth rises by increasing the replacement percentage. This test is widely accepted as a valid method for assessing not-stabilized adobes in areas of moderate rain (i.e. approx. 600 mm per year). The criteria of evaluation does not consider the loss of weight of samples which shows the opposite trend (i.e. the more the PPR addition, the lower weight loss) (Fig. 10). 

Regardless of the satisfactory mechanical and thermal results obtained in this research, the unfired earth bricks may pose a serious health risk. The soil may contain toxic metals which, due to the lack of mineral transformation at high temperature, might be a source of risk for inhabitants of adobe buildings. Some studies have revealed the risk of building with contaminated soil from mining activities e.g. Bolivia [57] or Peru [58]. Hence, with the aim of assessing the feasibility of using the tested adobes, a toxicity characteristic leaching procedure has been performed. From Table 4 it can be observed that concentrations of major elements are far from the regulatory levels stated by EPA-TCLP [37].
